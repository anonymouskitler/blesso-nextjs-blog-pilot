Two formats often get lumped together as "video interviews." In a one-way interview, candidates record answers to a fixed set of questions. In a conversational AI video interview, the interviewer responds to what the candidate says. Neither is automatically better. The useful question is which one gives your team what it needs to review candidates fairly.

This comparison uses [HappierHire](https://www.happierhire.ai/) as the example of the conversational model. Product details below come from its [official site](https://www.happierhire.ai/), and you should confirm them there before relying on them.

## What each format does

A one-way interview asks every candidate the same questions in the same order. Your team watches recordings afterwards. That is simple, and the sameness is easy to explain.

According to HappierHire's site, its AI interviewer "listens, understands context, and asks intelligent follow-up questions," and evaluates answers against a custom rubric. Candidates are told up front that they are speaking with an AI.

## Five things to compare

### 1. Follow-up depth

A fixed list cannot probe a vague answer. If a candidate says "we improved the process," the next recorded question is still the next recorded question. An adaptive interviewer can ask what changed and who did it. The trade-off is consistency: follow-ups differ between candidates, so check that your rubric, not the conversation's path, drives the comparison.

### 2. Candidate experience

Both formats let candidates interview without scheduling a live call. HappierHire says interviews run anytime, and that each candidate gets a unique link, added individually or by CSV. A conversation may feel more natural than talking to a prompt, but that is something to test with your own candidates. Ask them, and do not assume.

### 3. Evidence traceability

With recordings alone, reviewers scrub through video and take their own notes. HappierHire describes scores tied to timestamped evidence, with each score defined by the team's rubric, so a reviewer can jump to the moment behind a score. Whatever tool you use, ask whether every score points back to something a person can watch.

### 4. Reviewer control

Look at who defines "good." HappierHire says you paste a job description, receive a generated rubric, and can edit it for each role. It also says it flags responses that may be AI-generated, shown as a prompt to review the clip. Treat a flag as a reason to look, not a verdict. A person should decide what, if anything, it means.

### 5. Setup effort

Fixed questions are quick to write. A rubric takes more thought, since someone has to say what a strong answer looks like. That work is worth doing for either format.

## No winner, a better question

Fixed questions favor uniformity and simplicity. Adaptive conversation favors depth and a richer record. Which matters more depends on the role, the volume and how your reviewers work. This article does not claim one format produces better hires, and the sources above do not establish that.

## A practical pilot checklist

- Pick one role and have your team write its rubric before any interviews.
- Run a small group of candidates through each format, or compare against your current process.
- Have two reviewers score independently, then compare notes.
- Open the timestamped evidence behind several scores and check that it supports them.
- Review every suspicious-answer flag by hand, and record what you concluded.
- Collect candidate feedback on clarity and comfort.
- Confirm with your legal and HR advisers any notice, consent and data requirements that apply to you.

Whichever format you pick, the tool prepares evidence and people make the hiring decision.
